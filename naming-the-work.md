# Naming the Work: A Reference for Talking About What You Actually Do

*A study guide for recognizing, articulating, and eventually negotiating for the kind of cross-functional, ambiguity-absorbing work you do — as opposed to ticket-execution work.*

---

## Why this document exists

You don't pick up backlog tickets and execute what they say. You:

- Prototype solutions to determine whether something is even feasible before it becomes a ticket
- Dig through stale Confluence docs and chase down the right POC across 4-5 teams to get things approved
- Constantly answer "who should I ask, and will they actually know?"
- Convert ambiguous, open-ended problems into scoped, timeboxed tickets with a clean Definition of Done
- Do all of this in parallel, across teams, so other engineers can execute without you in the room

This work is real, valuable, and has names. If you can't name it, you can't put it on a resume, defend it in a leveling conversation, or recognize it as leverage. This doc gives you the vocabulary, the sources behind it, and a way to map it onto your own week.

---

## 1. Glue Work

**Source:** Tanya Reilly (former Staff Systems Engineer, Google; later Principal Engineer, Squarespace) — talk and essay titled *"Being Glue."*

**What it is:** The essential, often invisible work that holds a team or org together but doesn't show up as a shipped feature — unblocking people, reviewing designs, onboarding, tracking down answers, keeping institutional knowledge alive. Reilly's core argument: this is **technical leadership**, not a distraction from it, and it's expected of senior engineers even though it rarely gets scoped or credited as work.

**The catch (important):** Reilly also tells a cautionary story — an engineer who does glue work exceptionally well, gets glowing manager feedback, and *still* gets passed over for promotion because the formal criteria only reward shipped code. Her conclusion: glue work is real leverage, but it is invisible by default. **If you don't name it and track it, it doesn't count.**

**Your version of this:** Digging through Confluence, chasing down POCs, holding context across teams so nothing drops between them.

**Action item:** Start a running log (even a rough one) of the unblocks, approvals, and cross-team saves you personally drove. You'll need this evidence later — not for your current org, but for interviews and future leveling conversations.

## 2. Boundary Spanning / Brokerage

**Source:** Ronald Burt, sociologist — theory of *structural holes* (from his books *Structural Holes* and *Brokerage and Closure*).

**What it is:** A *structural hole* is a gap between two people or groups who hold complementary information but aren't connected to each other. The person who sits across that gap — connecting groups that otherwise wouldn't talk — is a **broker**. Burt argues brokers get three concrete advantages:

1. Access to more diverse information → fuels better ideas
2. Earlier access to information → they know things before others do
3. Control over how information flows → they become a chokepoint others depend on

**The evidence:** In an empirical study of a real company, Burt found that compensation, positive performance reviews, promotions, and highly-rated ideas were *disproportionately* concentrated among people whose networks spanned structural holes — i.e., the brokers.

**Your version of this:** "Who should I ask about this, and will they know?" is the brokerage question, asked daily, across 4-5 teams. You are the connective tissue between groups whose knowledge doesn't otherwise overlap.

**A useful related idea — the "brokerage ladder":** newer research building on Burt's work found that senior, high-output people are disproportionately the ones occupying brokerage positions — the shift from "does the work inside one team" to "spans gaps between teams" is a documented marker of seniority, not a detour from it.

## 3. Staff Engineer Archetypes

**Source:** Will Larson, *Staff Engineer: Leadership Beyond the Management Track* — the standard reference framework for Staff+ IC work.

Larson identifies four archetypes. Two map directly onto you:

| Archetype | Definition | Where you show up |
|---|---|---|
| **Tech Lead** | Guides the approach and execution of a particular team or focused area, partnering closely with the manager(s) involved | Scoping work, refining requirements, writing clean Definitions of Done for other engineers to execute against |
| **Solver** | Digs deep into arbitrarily complex, often ambiguous problems and finds a workable path forward — some stay on one area for a long time, others move hotspot to hotspot | Prototyping to determine feasibility *before* anything becomes a ticket |
| Architect | Owns direction/quality/approach for a critical area long-term | Not really you (yet) — this is longer-horizon system ownership |
| Right Hand | Extends an executive's scope and authority, operating as a trusted proxy | Not really you — this is closer to a TPM/chief-of-staff pattern tied to a specific leader |

**Why this is the right frame, not "senior engineer":** A Senior Engineer is typically handed a backlog and a Definition of Done. You are the one *producing* both, for other people, across teams, in parallel. That generation step — ambiguous problem → validated feasibility → scoped ticket → assigned owner — is the actual boundary between "executes work" and "creates and routes work." That boundary is where Staff/TPM-flavored roles live.

**One honest caveat:** these archetypes describe patterns that emerge from *earned* trust and influence over time — they're not a checklist you can complete to "become" one. Use them as a vocabulary for what you're already doing, not a target to perform toward.

## 4. The Compensation Reality (read this before your next comp conversation)

- Staff-tier roles generally out-earn Senior roles who purely execute: **15–30%+ total comp premium** is a commonly cited range, and at large tech companies the gap can run **$150K–$300K+ in total compensation**.
- Burt's data backs this up structurally, not just by title: brokerage position (not title) correlated with higher comp, better reviews, and more promotions.
- **But the money follows the *recognized level*, not the work itself.** This is the exact trap from Reilly's talk: you can do Staff-scope work indefinitely at Senior-scope pay if nobody formally sees it.
- The blunt version, from a recruiter writing about this exact pattern: *if you're the person three teams quietly ask before they make a call, your employer is already getting staff-level work at a senior-level price — the market eventually corrects that, usually by a competitor poaching you.*

**Implication for you right now:** you're changing roles. This is the highest-leverage moment to convert unrecognized scope into a recognized title/level, because you're not asking your current org to re-evaluate you — you're describing your scope fresh, to someone with no anchor on your old title.

## 5. How to Actually Say This (cheat sheet for resumes/interviews)

Avoid: *"Ran scrum ceremonies and gave status updates."*

Use instead, mixing and matching:

- "Drove feasibility triage across ambiguous problems — prototyping solutions before scoping them into parallelizable, clearly-defined work for 2-3 teams simultaneously."
- "Acted as a cross-functional broker across 4-5 teams, identifying the right subject-matter owners and unblocking approvals that otherwise stalled in outdated documentation."
- "Converted open-ended technical questions into timeboxed tickets with clear acceptance criteria and Definition of Done, enabling parallel execution across multiple engineering teams."
- "Functioned as a de facto technical lead / solver across cross-team initiatives — validating feasibility, defining scope, and routing execution to the appropriate owners."

Try mapping **one real recent example** (a specific spike, approval, or cross-team save) to each bullet above. Concrete examples beat abstract descriptions every time in interviews.
